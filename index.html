THE CURRENT PICTURE · UNITED STATES

The story is larger than mass shootings.

In 2024, more than 44,000 people in the U.S. died from firearm injuries—about 121 a day. More than half of those deaths were suicides; about one-third were homicides.

This is the latest complete annual mortality snapshot cited on CDC’s page, updated September 2, 2026. It covers firearm deaths broadly—not a count of public mass shootings. CDC: Fast Facts on Firearm Injury and Death ↗

WHAT THE EVIDENCE CAN EXPLAIN

There is no single cause.

Rare, high-impact events are not explained by one trait, diagnosis, or motive label. Research can identify conditions that change risk and opportunities to intervene; this archive cannot diagnose a person or establish why an event happened.

01 / ACCESS & LETHALITY

Access changes the stakes of a crisis.

A systematic review of 16 observational studies found household firearm access associated with higher odds of completed suicide and homicide victimization. That is population-level evidence about those outcomes—not a causal explanation for public mass shootings or an individual prediction.

Anglemyer et al., 2014 · systematic review ↗
02 / CONTEXT, NOT A PROFILE

Understand circumstances without stereotyping.

CDC’s National Violent Death Reporting System links death certificates with medical examiner/coroner and law-enforcement records to document circumstances and life stressors. Those context fields can guide prevention research; they are not deterministic warning signs or proof of motive.

CDC · National Violent Death Reporting System ↗
03 / DEFINITIONS SHAPE THE PICTURE

Different questions create different counts.

“Firearm death,” “school-associated violent death,” “active shooter,” and “public mass shooting” are not interchangeable categories. Thresholds, settings, victim counts, and exclusions vary. Compare only sources with compatible definitions and time periods.

NCES · definitions and school data ↗

The preserved table’s “Possible Motive” and mental-health fields are source labels with undocumented coding and incomplete context. Do not treat them as clinical findings, predictive profiles, or causal evidence.

POLICY, POLITICS & PUBLIC SAFETY

Ask what works—and for whom.

The short answer: some gun laws are associated with reductions in specific kinds of harm. The evidence does not establish a universal effect on “attacks,” and it cannot be reduced to a party label.

GUN LAWS · FIREARM SUICIDE

Some specific laws show promising, outcome-specific evidence.

A 2026 county-level study covering 637 counties and 2000–2023 found lower firearm-suicide rates associated with five state-law categories: handgun-purchase permits, minimum purchase age 21, child-access protections, violent-misdemeanor purchase prohibitions, and laws allowing law enforcement to petition for extreme risk protection orders. It is an observational study; the authors call for further confirmation.

Azevedo & Siegel, 2026 · study and methods ↗
BACKGROUND CHECKS · FIREARM HOMICIDE

“Background checks” are not one uniform intervention.

A separate 2026 study of point-of-sale comprehensive checks in six states found no adjusted association with county firearm-homicide rates for 2000–2021. That does not test every background-check design or negate evidence for purchase-permit systems; implementation and state context matter.

Schleimer et al., 2026 · study and methods ↗
PUBLIC MASS SHOOTINGS · SCHOOL ATTACKS

We cannot confidently say which laws reduce these rare events.

Most law evaluations measure suicide, homicide, or child injury—not public mass shootings. Those outcomes are not interchangeable. The events are rare, definitions vary, and evidence about the effect of specific laws on these events remains limited and mixed.

PARTY & GOVERNMENT LEVEL

No credible “best party” ranking follows from these records.

The archive has no law, party-control, ICE-activity, or post-2016 outcome series. A raw count by president or party would confound national trends, population, state laws, timing, and event definitions. Congress writes federal statutes and funding; the executive enforces them; states and localities shape many laws and prevention programs. The office matters through specific decisions—not a party label alone.

ICE · ENFORCEMENT IS NOT A SHOOTING OUTCOME

Evidence points to a reporting and trust trade-off—not a demonstrated effect on mass shootings.

A 2024 NBER study of the Secure Communities program found reduced crime reporting by Hispanic victims and increased victimization; total reported crime did not change. A separate survey experiment found lower trust in local police when they cooperated with ICE. These studies concern specific interior-enforcement contexts and broader public safety—not firearm attacks or every current ICE operation. ICE’s public ERO dashboards report enforcement activity (arrests, detention, removals) through December 31, 2024; activity counts alone do not measure crimes prevented or caused.

Gonçalves, Jácome & Weisburst, 2024 · Secure Communities and public safety ↗ Wong et al., 2021 · trust experiment ↗ ICE · Enforcement and Removal Operations statistics ↗
HISTORY & CAUSATION

Timelines raise hypotheses; comparisons test them.

Elections, law changes, enforcement shifts, the pandemic, economic shocks, and changes in firearm access can overlap. Their timing alone cannot show what caused an attack or a change in rates. A fair test needs consistent definitions, population-adjusted outcomes, policy start dates and implementation, pre-trend checks, comparable places, and plausible time lags. The local archive cannot supply these inputs.

RAND · how evidence varies by policy and outcome ↗

Bottom line: some gun laws are linked to lower rates of specific kinds of firearm harm, but findings differ by law, outcome, and implementation. Current research does not justify attributing attack counts to a president, political party, ICE, or one social event—especially from this incomplete 1966–2016 archive.

FROM UNDERSTANDING TO PREVENTION

Build safety at every level.

The strongest approach is a portfolio: reduce immediate access to lethal means, support people and communities under strain, and evaluate policies against clearly defined outcomes.

PERSONAL & HOUSEHOLD SAFETY

Make moments of crisis survivable.

Secure storage can prevent unauthorized access, including by children and people at risk of harming themselves or others. Connect people to timely, voluntary support and crisis care.

CDC guidance · broad firearm injury and suicide prevention
COMMUNITY RESPONSE

Interrupt conflict and connect people to care.

Street outreach, hospital-based violence intervention, and suicide-prevention supports can create opportunities to help. CDC describes promising results; effectiveness depends on local design, reach, and evaluation.

Promising approaches · measure locally
HEALTHIER CONDITIONS

Invest in conditions that help people thrive.

Housing and economic supports, safe public spaces, and reliable routes to school address conditions associated with violence and stress. These are public-health strategies—not guarantees against a specific rare event.

CDC prevention framework · outcomes vary by approach
POLICY & ACCOUNTABILITY

Ask what works, for whom, and by which measure.

Define the outcome first—suicide, homicide, nonfatal injury, school safety, or public mass shootings—then track implementation, population denominators, equity, and unintended effects over time.

Evidence rule · never transfer an effect across outcomes without testing

Scope matters: most prevention evidence cited here concerns firearm injury, suicide, homicide, or community violence broadly. It should not be presented as proof that a particular intervention prevents public mass shootings specifically.

READ WITH CARE

Use evidence.
Reject easy answers.

The local archive is a preserved project snapshot, not a live incident census; its schema, provenance, coverage, and collection methodology have not been independently validated. Current public-health and prevention resources are linked directly from their publishers; each has its own definitions, release schedule, and limits. Nothing is silently merged, and the archive does not establish why a person acted or whether an intervention prevented an event.

The latest complete annual mortality figure cited here is for 2024; provisional national data have a reporting lag. Reopen the linked primary sources for updated figures. The repository’s Excel filename is a small macOS metadata sidecar, not a usable workbook, so no spreadsheet download is offered.

Research limitations. Counts describe different populations and event definitions; associations are not individual predictions or proof of causation. Prevention evidence for suicide, homicide, or broader firearm injury is not automatically evidence about rare public mass shootings. The interactive database runs locally; source files remain unchanged.
